# 2.7 Consensus Models

Every technology surveyed so far in this chapter is a library or platform choice. This section is different: it surveys *claim-verification consensus models* — the algorithmic and organizational patterns by which a group decides whether a disputed statement is true — because FactStamp's core value proposition is not any single library, it is the consensus mechanism itself. Getting this choice right (or wrong) matters more to the project's credibility than any frontend framework decision.

## 2.7.1 Survey of Consensus Models

**(a) Single-moderator / editorial decision.** This is how traditional fact-checking organizations such as Snopes, PolitiFact, or India-focused outlets like AltNews operate: a professional editorial team investigates a claim and an editor (or small editorial board) publishes a final verdict. The strength of this model is depth — a trained investigator can pursue primary sources, contact involved parties, and apply institutional editorial standards. Its weakness, discussed further in Section 2.7.3, is throughput: one team cannot scale to the volume of hyper-local, informal claims circulating on a platform like WhatsApp, and the decision process is opaque to the public — readers see the verdict, not the deliberation.

**(b) Simple majority vote.** Any group of N reviewers each casts a verdict, and whichever verdict has the most votes wins, full stop. This is easy to implement and easy to explain, but it has an obvious flaw: it treats every voter identically regardless of their track record, and it says nothing about *how* certain the group actually is — a 2-1 split and a 10-1 split both just resolve to "the majority verdict," with no confidence signal attached.

**(c) Byzantine-fault-tolerant (BFT) / blockchain-style distributed consensus.** Protocols in this family (e.g., PBFT, or blockchain consensus mechanisms like Proof-of-Stake) are designed so that a distributed set of nodes can agree on a single value even when some fraction of participants are actively malicious or faulty, typically requiring a supermajority (commonly greater than 2/3) of participants to agree before a value is finalized. This is the right tool when the participants are mutually distrusting, anonymous nodes in an open network and the thing being agreed on is a ledger state. It is a poor fit for claim verification specifically: BFT consensus is about agreeing on *which value was submitted*, not about *weighing the quality of the evidence behind competing values* — it has no native concept of "this participant has historically been more accurate" or "this source citation is more credible than that one," which is precisely the information a fact-check needs to weigh.

**(d) Weighted quorum consensus.** A minimum number of independent reviewers (a quorum) must participate before a verdict is finalized, and the final confidence in that verdict is a weighted function of multiple signals — not just the raw vote count. This model keeps majority voting's transparency (every reviewer's individual verdict and reasoning stays visible) while adding the two things pure majority voting lacks: a minimum-participation floor before any verdict can be trusted at all, and a mechanism for letting evidence quality and reviewer track record influence the final confidence score rather than treating every vote as interchangeable.

## 2.7.2 FactStamp's Choice — Weighted Quorum Consensus

FactStamp implements model (d): a minimum 3-verifier quorum with a weighted confidence formula, computed by `calculateConfidenceScore()` in `src/lib/confidenceScore.ts`:

$$\text{Confidence} = 0.40 \times \text{AgreementRatio} + 0.30 \times \text{AvgVerifierReputation} + 0.30 \times \text{SourceQualityScore}$$

No verdict is finalized until at least three independent verifications exist — this quorum floor is what a pure majority vote lacks, and it exists specifically to prevent a single verifier (or two colluding accounts) from unilaterally deciding a claim's fate. Once quorum is reached, the three weighted components combine agreement, track record, and evidence quality into a single number rather than treating a vote as a flat, interchangeable unit:

- **AgreementRatio (40%)** — the proportion of participating verifiers whose verdict matches the eventual majority verdict, computed from the raw `verdict` values submitted with each verification.
- **AvgVerifierReputation (30%)** — the mean `verifierReputation` across all participating verifiers, so a claim reviewed by several high-reputation verifiers is weighted more confidently than the same vote split among brand-new accounts.
- **SourceQualityScore (30%)** — the mean of each verification's `sourceQuality` score, so a verdict backed by citations to authoritative primary sources scores higher confidence than one backed by unlinked or low-credibility sources, even at an identical agreement ratio. `determineSourceQuality()` and `sourceQualityToScore()` in the same file classify each cited source URL against curated high-quality domain sets (e.g., `who.int`, `pib.gov.in`, `rbi.org.in`) and mid-quality domain sets (e.g., major national news outlets), scoring unlisted domains lowest.

This is deliberately not a BFT-style protocol, because FactStamp's participants are not anonymous, mutually-distrusting nodes agreeing on a single submitted value — they are named, reputation-tracked community members whose historical accuracy is exactly the signal the confidence formula wants to use. Reusing a supermajority-of-anonymous-nodes protocol here would throw away the reputation and source-quality signal the project actually has available.

## 2.7.3 Supporting Mechanisms: Duplicate Pre-Filtering and Consensus-Deadline Expiry

Two supporting mechanisms make the weighted quorum model workable in practice rather than purely theoretical:

**Jaccard-similarity duplicate detection as a pre-filter.** Before a new submission enters the verification queue at all, `findDuplicate()` in `src/lib/duplicateDetection.ts` compares its normalized, tokenized text against existing claims using Jaccard similarity — $J(A, B) = |A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$ — computed over word tokens longer than 3 characters. A match with similarity $\ge 0.75$ (the `threshold` parameter of `findDuplicate()`) redirects the submitter to the existing claim's verdict instead of opening a new, duplicate verification queue entry. This exists precisely because the single-moderator model's throughput problem (see Section 2.7.1a) would resurface even in a community-quorum model without it: identical viral forwards worded slightly differently would otherwise each need their own independent 3-verifier quorum, multiplying verifier workload for no additional evidentiary value.

**Automatic 7-day consensus-deadline expiry.** A claim that fails to reach a clear majority verdict within 7 days automatically transitions to a `CONTESTED` state rather than remaining in limbo indefinitely. This addresses a failure mode none of the other three models in Section 2.7.1 solve on their own: a single-moderator model can simply take as long as it needs (at the cost of throughput), a pure majority vote has no time dimension at all, and a BFT protocol assumes participants keep proposing until agreement is reached. A community-quorum model with unpaid, occasional volunteer participation has no such guarantee — verifiers may never reach three, or may split evenly and never converge — so an explicit deadline is what keeps every claim moving toward a terminal, user-visible state instead of quietly stalling in the review queue forever.
